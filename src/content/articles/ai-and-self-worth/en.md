A few days ago, I finished teaching an AI course that was part of a larger project to prepare and train everyone at our company. Overall, I was happy with what I presented and the feedback I received. But in the rush of delivering the course, I never found time to share something very important with my colleagues. This is the thing I left unsaid:

People asked me and the other speakers questions like these:

1. How do we stay up to date? I feel as though I'm falling behind the world of AI.
2. Has AI already replaced humans anywhere in the world?
3. Which technology or tool should we invest our time in? Which has a better future?

For me, these questions carry another question, a deeper concern. I think some of them have a hidden anxiety at their roots. Worry about a real change, about a future that hasn't happened yet and that we are already wrestling with in our minds. This is my reading of it: consider whether, when we ask “What should I learn?”, we might actually be looking for something other than an educational recommendation. We want to know what to learn so we can finally feel at ease.

The more I think about it, the more I realize that “How do I stay up to date?” isn't a new question. Years ago, programmers with less experience than me were asking the same thing. In this technology industry, it almost seems natural to be forever worried about the shift from one thing to another! But you know, and you've surely experienced it, that when we're constantly worried about falling behind, even choosing the next step becomes difficult. Whatever we choose, we're thinking at the same time about everything we didn't choose.

I hear that same familiar worry in these questions now. Someone who hasn't yet had time to find their footing in this profession is confronted with news about everything they've learned becoming useless. Energy that could go into learning and experimenting goes instead into asking whether anything is still worth learning at all!

Let me say it again: I don't think the main problem is always that you're out of date. In fact, please don't keep up. Please, let yourself not know. Please learn, more and more, how to live with uncertainty and an unclear future.

You cannot keep track of everything amid this volume of changes, developments and news. Even if you can spend several hours a day staying up to date, tomorrow there will be something new you don't know. If the idea is that all this knowledge will finally put your fear of falling behind to rest, it probably won't do you much good. You also need some time left to use what you've learned.

To some extent, I see the worry behind the other questions in the same way. A worry about the present and the future to which the companies leading the AI race and technology investors generously, enthusiastically gift fresh fuel every day. We can stay trapped in the cycle of falling behind while they get richer.

Some days, what gets served up in the news sounds more like reports of an army of aliens from another solar system invading Earth. Which agent has broken into more systems, which model has beaten whom, and which profession is next to be finished off. Among these stories, figuring out how each development relates to our own actual problems isn't easy.

Under these conditions, concentrating and spending time on understanding concepts, solving problems and learning deeply becomes difficult. Whatever you give your time and attention to, you feel you're behind something else, or someone else! These impressionable, ever-comparing brains of ours.

The course I taught deliberately focused on the core, foundational concepts of artificial intelligence and machine learning, rather than the outer layers of applications and tools. Interestingly, some people said in their feedback that they had expected to learn more about using the tools. Some probably felt they were learning calculus they would never use. They may genuinely have needed more practical training; their feedback, of course, isn't in itself a sign of anxiety. But my choice was to devote a substantial part of the time to understanding things that I hoped would still help when we encountered the next tool.

And let me return to a fundamental question: if a tool can do the work we're paid to do today better than we can, what do we conclude about ourselves? That we need to learn a skill or change direction? Or that we're no longer any use? The distance between those two matters to me. Losing a job and an income is a real and serious problem. But if we've tied our worth entirely to our professional abilities, every new piece of news can threaten something beyond our position at work.

This article is turning into a lot of advice, I know, and I don't mind. I'd like to pass this on to the generation after mine: for you, your most important priority is yourself. You. With everything you know and everything you don't. Every time the market value of a skill changes, you don't have to put a new price on yourself all over again.

Let a flood sweep the world away; how you feel about yourself and how you care for yourself still matter. Knowing this won't stop the flood, but it might help your feet tremble and slip a little less. Fear won't make you let go of whatever you're gripping tightly to keep the flood from carrying you away. You'll probably make it to the edge of a roof, with the water rising almost as high as the rooftop. And if you're lucky and your pocket hasn't got wet, a cigarette up there would hit the spot.

Let's suppose that six months from now, robots will take your place. Go further: you're resting at home, and the robots come and throw you out. Right here in Tehran. It's an exaggerated scenario, I know; I want us to follow this frightening picture all the way through. So what about the time between today and then? What will you do? Do whatever you can to prevent it. But if you can't stop it, what will you do with those six months? Stay caught in anxiety and worry about that night?

Here is my choice: I'd rather be thrown out of my home while I'm doing my own work. Working on my own project. Reading my book. Drinking my coffee. Resting. Talking with someone I love, or sitting together and saying absolutely nothing. In the middle of this beautiful moment, I will be thrown out of my home. By something I'm only just learning about. For me, this is life itself.

I don't want to put my life on hold until the future has been settled. Learning and preparing are part of that life too, but they aren't supposed to be all of it.

I suggest we practice this. By not reading. By not knowing. But carrying on, and learning a little as we carry on and work. Knowing a little is enough for the next step. We don't understand something and get confused. All right. Right there, we understand just a little more and move forward again. As far as we can. Honestly, I think this is how humans have always lived. It's just that, now and then, when we reach a sharp bend, we forget it has always been this way. I've learned this from history. Reading history lets me watch a time-lapse of these events. I can zoom in or zoom out and see this way of living, this creature called a human being.

I thought of a video of Babak Karimi that was circulating on social media, in which he told a story about a blind person whose white cane only ever reveals the next meter ahead. What has stayed with me from that story is this: for the next step, that one meter of clarity is enough. We don't need a clear view of the distant horizon from here.
