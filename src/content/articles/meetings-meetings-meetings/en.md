Sometimes, halfway through a meeting, I realize that two people have been talking for twenty minutes and several of us, myself included, have been quiet. It may be a good conversation. One of them has noticed something the other missed. They trade examples and gradually work out where they disagree. What I wonder is whether the rest of us needed to be there for all twenty minutes. If those two had talked beforehand and written to tell us what they figured out, what would we have missed?

To get a sense of how much of our time this question touches, I looked at the last six sprints for part of our team. Four people logged a total of 333 person-hours in meetings, close to 22 percent of the work recorded for the group. One person spent about 16 percent of their logged time in meetings. For two others, the figure was over 27 percent.

My first thought was that we have too many meetings. I went looking for a number to test that impression against. In a study of more than 9,000 workers in Norway, Deming and his colleagues found that people spent about 12 percent of their working hours in meetings on average. Our figure was close to 22 percent. That gap makes me curious about what happened in those 333 hours. Did each of those four people really need to spend that much time there? Of course, Deming and his colleagues asked people across different occupations about their working hours. I’m looking at time logged by four people on a technical team. How could I conclude from that comparison that we’d work better if we got our figure down to 12 percent tomorrow? I have a more concrete question. If I wanted to shorten even one of those meetings, which part would I cut, and why?[^1]

Back to those twenty minutes of silence. I might be quiet because tomorrow I’ll have to carry out the decision we make today, and I want to hear why we ruled out one of the options. I might have spotted a problem and been unable to get a word in. There’s a simpler possibility, too. Maybe I was invited because I’m on the team and nobody stopped to think which part of the discussion concerned me. From the outside, all three look much the same. That’s why, after a meeting, I’d rather ask someone who was quiet, “Which part was useful to you? Was there something you wanted to say and didn’t get a chance to?” Their answers would probably tell me more about how to run the next meeting than a tally of how many minutes each person spoke.

One finding caught my eye in the CIPD’s review of research on workplace meetings. In a study of 367 participants, people generally gave larger meetings lower ratings. An agenda was associated with better ratings when people could see it _before the meeting_. The study measured how participants perceived meeting quality. It gives me no magic number to put on an invitation list, but it does give me something to think about when I call a meeting. If someone knows beforehand exactly what we need to decide, perhaps they’re better placed than I am to judge whether to join from the start, come for part of it, or read the decision afterward.[^2]

Something else occurs to me in meetings. Why should the first time all of us encounter a problem be right here, while the meeting is underway?

Take a hypothetical case. We’re going to discuss a system that reads administrative and legal documents and suggests who should receive them. We’ve compared its suggestions with the people the documents actually went to, and its accuracy looks low. Before the meeting, someone could put a few wrong suggestions next to the documents and see whether they could have identified the recipient from the information the model had. The person who wrote the evaluation could check a particular case, too. If a document went to two people and the model correctly suggested one of them, what does our “accuracy” figure do with that?

Now, when we sit down together, we can start with that very document or letter. Someone familiar with how documents are routed in the organization might take one look and say, “To figure out who gets this, you need to know something the document never tells you.” Here I can see a reason to be in the same room. Each of us has seen a part of the problem that could change how someone else understands it. And if it turns out the problem was just in the calculation, perhaps the person who wrote the evaluation can clear it up for everyone with a single example.

In an experiment published in _Management Science_, Girotra, Terwiesch, and Ulrich compared two ways of generating ideas. Some groups worked together from the outset. In others, people thought independently before gathering as a group. The latter groups generated more ideas, came up with better ones, and were better at recognizing their quality. The experiment was about generating ideas. What I’d like to try in our meetings is that small change in the order of things. Someone who’s had a chance to look at the problem before hearing my take might bring us an explanation we’d otherwise miss. If I’d spoken first, I might have sent their thoughts in a different direction. Especially if I’m the one leading the team![^3]

## Meetings in the Age of AI

What does having a machine that can reason and draw inferences mean for the way we hold meetings? I wonder whether AI could take us to a point where we no longer need to get together to make technical decisions, work on designs, or come up with ideas. Could attending a meeting gradually become a kind of performance, where we say things out loud simply so everyone knows what’s going to happen?

I doubt AI will make us so self-sufficient, so individualistic, that we’ll lose the need to come together to make decisions. This is still our territory. We’re still responsible for what we do with AI. We still have to be able to defend it, make a case for it within the economic structures we’ve built, and reckon with the consequences of our decisions. In fact, I think AI can make our meetings richer in two ways, and help us dispense with the ones we can do without.

1. Before a meeting, each of us has access to AI. We can think through the problem independently, consult the model, and come up with explanations of our own. The people who gather have already formed some ideas. We can spend our time finding out where those ideas diverge. What matters to me is that each person gets to encounter the problem for themselves and explore it with AI in their own way. In an experiment on short-story writing, Doshi and Hauser found that readers gave higher ratings to stories by writers who had access to AI-generated ideas, but the stories also grew more like one another. Writing fiction is a different activity from investigating a system error. Even so, it gives me a reason to jot down what I’ve seen and what puzzles me before I ask the model what it thinks.[^4]

2. One big thing AI has done for us is bring down the cost of implementation. We might have spent a meeting discussing how we’d design and build a solution. Now we can ask AI to build it beforehand and bring a working example for everyone to see and think through. Getting from an idea to something we could try used to take a substantial investment of time. That made us deliberate, sometimes painstakingly so, about settling on a design. We might spend several meetings on the architecture of a single part of a product. Today, getting even an unverified first result can cost us some tokens where it used to cost us that time. That could considerably shorten the distance between calling a meeting and getting something out of it.

## One last thought

I wonder what other social animals gather for and what they do on their own. I’d like to explore that question with AI and see what the list looks like for chimpanzees, bonobos, or crows. I suspect it would make for an interesting comparison with an animal of the genus _Homo_. It might even be of some use to that animal.

For one of our next meetings, I want to try this on a small scale. I’ll write down the question beforehand, ask the people with relevant data or experience for a few lines on how they see it, and start the meeting where our accounts differ or a decision is still open. If reading those few lines clears things up, perhaps a conversation between two people and a written update for the others will do. When we work out how much time it took, though, we should count the preparation and the reading, too. If shortening a meeting means giving four people homework and then reading it all aloud anyway, we haven’t gained much.

I still don’t know how many of those 333 person-hours we could have spent better. But the next time I notice that several people have been quiet for twenty minutes, I have a question for myself before I ask them to participate more. When I invited them, what did I think their presence would change about this conversation, or what did I hope they’d take away from hearing it?
